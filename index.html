Why Traction Matters

Traction safety should be part of the design, not the recovery plan.

Most traction decisions get made after something goes wrong. This page is about what changes when they get made earlier.

Slips, trips, and falls are among the most common sources of workplace injury, and among the most preventable. They keep happening because traction usually enters the conversation too late to influence the outcome. The science is clear and the products exist. The timing is the problem.

The category, reframed

How traction usually gets decided.

Late. The conversation tends to start after a slip or an audit finding. By the time it gets going, the project is built and the budget is locked, and traction stops being a design choice and becomes a line item someone has to defend.

How it should get decided

As a piece of how the building works. Specified during design. Engineered for the conditions a floor actually sees. Defensible from day one, not bolted on after the first claim.

Why the timing keeps slipping

Traction is rarely owned by a single function. Safety and EHS leaders care about it most, but the decisions that shape it often happen earlier, in engineering, design, and procurement, before safety gets a meaningful vote. Engineers can treat traction as a finishing detail. Procurement reads it as a discretionary line item. Operations only feels its absence in incident reports and maintenance tickets. The people who care most about traction end up with the least leverage to specify it.

What "engineered traction" actually means

Engineered, not improvised.

Traction has to be engineered for the conditions a surface will actually see, the same way you would engineer load or span. That can mean raised metal dots welded into the material itself (ALGRIP®). It can also mean a metal grit sprayed into it during fabrication (OnGrip®). Either way the traction is manufactured in. What it should not mean is hoping a generic finish, an off-the-shelf coating, or a housekeeping policy will make a slippery floor safe.

How we evaluate environments

  • Wet, greasy, and washdown conditions
  • High-traffic and high-abrasion areas
  • Mixed-condition zones like entrances, transitions, and crossovers
  • Compliance-sensitive public-access areas

Where traction belongs in the buying process

Earlier than most teams think.

There is a moment in every project, somewhere between concept and specification, where traction can be added at almost no incremental cost. There is another moment, much later, when adding it becomes a budget event. The most useful question a project team can ask is which of those moments they are currently in.

A simple sequence to follow

  1. Define the operating environment. What does the floor actually see? Water, grease, traffic, abrasion, chemistry.
  2. Match the surface to the environment. Engineered traction surfaces are not interchangeable. Selection is application-specific.
  3. Specify it. Do not assume it. Make traction a line item, not an unspoken design intent.
  4. Build defensibility into the record. Standards alignment, performance documentation, and consistent decision logic create a record that holds up under scrutiny.
  5. Plan for change. Conditions evolve. Specify for the conditions the floor will see in year ten, not just the ones it opens with.

By role

Who owns traction safety?

Everyone with a meaningful stake in design, operations, safety, or capital decisions has a role. The useful question is what each function should be asking.

Safety and EHS leaders

Ask whether each traction decision could be defended six months from now. If the answer is unclear, traction has not been specified. It has been hoped for.

Engineers and specifiers

Ask whether traction is a design variable in your current projects or a downstream correction. Specifying it early is one of the more reliable ways to prevent late-stage change orders.

Operations and facilities

Ask what the maintenance and downtime profile of your current traction approach actually looks like. Recurring complaints and repeated stopgaps are signs of an engineering problem, not a behavior problem.

Procurement and finance

Ask what total cost of ownership looks like across the life of the surface. Unit price is almost never the most important number.

Products · ALGRIP®

ALGRIP® is traction welded into the surface itself.

A patented laser deposition process bonds metal traction features directly to metal grating or plate flooring. The result is engineered, durable slip resistance in wet, oily, or dirty conditions.

The traction features are welded into the substrate during fabrication, which is why ALGRIP® holds up where epoxy aggregates, painted grit, and adhesive finishes age out. And because the texture is made of discrete raised dots rather than a continuous rough field, it cleans more easily than a grit surface does. That combination is why it leads in food and beverage.

How it works

A patented laser deposition process.

Small metal traction features are welded directly to the surface of metal grating or plate. Shoes and tires encircle and grab those features, raising the dynamic coefficient of friction (DCOF) in every direction of travel. Because the features are welded to the substrate rather than bonded with adhesive or sprayed on as a layer, they perform through years of wear, cleaning chemistry, and chemical exposure.

The result is traction that behaves like a property of the surface, because it is.

How it works

A thermal spray application.

A thermal application method sprays metal grit onto carbon steel, stainless steel, or aluminum, raising the coefficient of friction across plate, stair treads, and bar grating. The texture is continuous and coarse, so it bites in every direction of travel. It performs in slippery, wet, and oily conditions, and it stays put under heavy traffic, which is where epoxy aggregate and painted grit finishes give up.

OnGrip® is engineered. It is not a paint, not a tape, and not a hopeful coating.

Food and Beverage Facility · Wet floor / processing area

Why food and beverage demands an engineered approach

The structural realities.

Food and beverage environments share a set of conditions that make traction performance hard to negotiate around. Standing water and condensate are part of regular operations. Protein, fat, and oil residues change the friction profile of the floor throughout the day. Washdown cycles introduce additional chemistry on a daily or shift-based cadence. Foot and equipment traffic wears surfaces unevenly. These are not edge cases. They are the everyday operating environment.

Why behavior change alone is not the answer

Footwear policies, signage, housekeeping rounds, and training all matter. They also have limits. In a wet processing environment, disciplined behavior cannot fully overcome a surface that was never specified for the conditions. Engineering controls, including surface selection, sit higher on the hierarchy of controls than behavioral controls for a reason.

Where traction decisions most often go wrong

Five patterns we see repeatedly.

  1. Traction gets left out of the original specification. Engineering treats the floor as a substrate choice and assumes traction will follow.
  2. Sanitation is asked to solve a slip problem it cannot solve. Cleaning frequency goes up, complaints continue, and the root cause never gets addressed.
  3. After an incident, a fast retrofit gets selected on price. The fix works for a quarter, then ages out under washdown chemistry, leaving the original problem and a depleted budget.
  4. Each shift adapts informally. Mats here, signage there, employee workarounds everywhere. The facility ends up with a patchwork no one fully understands.
  5. By the time procurement is involved, the cheapest option looks like the safest one. It almost never is.

Oils, coolants, heavy traffic, and mixed work zones make traction a real engineering problem in manufacturing facilities. The good news is that it is a solvable one.

Why manufacturing demands an engineered approach

The conditions a manufacturing floor actually sees.

Most manufacturing facilities share a set of conditions that single-source flooring rarely handles well. Oils, coolants, and hydraulic fluids are part of normal operations, and they migrate. Forklift and pallet jack traffic abrades the surface in concentrated paths. Welding spatter, paint overspray, and grit from outside loading docks all change surface behavior over time. Different work zones have different conditions, but the same floor often runs through all of them.

Why behavior change alone is not the answer

Spill response programs, footwear policies, and housekeeping rounds all matter. They also have limits. In an environment where contamination is built into normal operations, traction is not a hygiene problem. It is an engineering problem.

Where traction decisions most often go wrong

Five patterns we see in manufacturing.

  1. Traction gets left to the flooring contractor. Whatever finish the substrate gets is what the facility lives with.
  2. Anti-slip mats are deployed as the primary engineering control. They migrate, wear out, and create their own trip hazards.
  3. Spill response is asked to solve a leak problem. The leak is the engineering issue. The slip is the symptom.
  4. Behavioral training carries the weight of preventing slips the surface should be handling. OSHA does not see it that way, and neither does a jury.
  5. Capital projects upgrade equipment but not the floor underneath it. New cell, same surface, same problem.

Hotel Lobby / Retail Floor

Why hospitality and public access need an engineered approach

The realities of a customer-facing surface.

Hospitality and public-access environments share a set of conditions that put traction on a particularly visible stage. Footwear is uncontrolled. Foot traffic is high and unpredictable. Outdoor-to-indoor transitions move water onto interior surfaces with every weather event. Mixed flooring materials (tile, polished concrete, terrazzo, wood) all behave differently when wet. Aesthetic expectations are higher than in most industrial settings, so a surface that performs but looks wrong tends to get replaced anyway.

ADA defensibility and public liability

Public-access surfaces sit at the intersection of ADA expectations, local building codes, and general premises liability. The defensible answer in this category is rarely the cheapest surface that passes inspection on the day it was installed. It is the surface that still performs and still tells a clear story when an incident is investigated two years later.

Where traction decisions most often go wrong

Five patterns we see in hospitality and public access.

  1. Aesthetics drive specification and traction is treated as a separate, lesser problem. The result is a floor that looks good and performs poorly under realistic conditions.
  2. Anti-slip mats and signage are the engineering control. Both depend on staff to be effective, and both create their own liability.
  3. Pool decks, kitchens, and entrances are treated as a single problem with a single solution. They are different problems and need to be specified that way.
  4. After a slip incident, a coating is sprayed over the existing surface. It wears, fails, and the original problem returns.
  5. The brand standard never includes traction performance. Each property handles it differently, and the chain ends up with the risk profile of its worst-prepared location.

Traction safety in transportation and infrastructure.

Train platforms, bus stations, airport walkways, marine decks, parking structures. The surfaces where the public moves under every kind of weather condition there is.

Transportation and infrastructure environments share a particular challenge. The surface has to perform outdoors, year-round, under public traffic the operator has no control over. Specification mistakes do not surface as customer complaints. They surface as injury claims, regulatory inspections, and headlines.

Explore our products Talk to a specialist
Transit Platform / Marine Gangway

Why transportation needs an engineered approach

The conditions outdoor public surfaces actually face.

Outdoor and semi-outdoor public surfaces share a set of conditions that defeat most off-the-shelf options. Freeze-thaw cycles attack adhesive layers and applied coatings. UV exposure changes how a surface looks and performs over time. Ice, snow, and de-icing chemistry add chemical exposure to mechanical wear. Foot traffic concentrates on stairs, ramps, platform edges, and access points. The same surface has to handle peak volume and low-light early-morning conditions, every day, for years.

Standards, inspections, and the regulatory layer

Transportation environments tend to be heavily inspected. DOT, FAA, FRA, USCG, port authorities, or local transit agencies all touch different assets. A defensible traction approach is not just about passing today's inspection. It is about a documented decision logic that holds up when the same surface gets inspected by a different agency three years from now.

Where traction decisions most often go wrong

Five patterns we see in transportation and infrastructure.

  1. Anti-slip tape is treated as a primary engineering control. It peels, wears, and creates trip hazards of its own.
  2. The surface gets replaced after every incident, like for like. The replacement should be specified against the condition that caused the incident, not against the surface that failed.
  3. Transitions between surfaces are specified separately from the main surface. Platform edges, gangways, ramp landings. They rarely match in performance.
  4. UV and freeze-thaw exposure are treated as inevitable rather than as specifiable design conditions. They are not inevitable. They are predictable.
  5. Maintenance contracts cover cleaning, not surface performance. The original specification is rarely defended over time.
